Memory

Why spaced repetition beats cramming

Mara Whitfield·12 min read

Most students study by re-reading. It feels productive, and it is almost useless. What works is unglamorous: review at widening intervals.

Spaced repetition exploits two quirks of memory. The first is that retrieval, not restudy, is what strengthens a memory: every time you pull something out of your head, the path back to it gets easier to find. The second is that a memory retrieved just as it is about to be forgotten is strengthened far more than one retrieved while it is still fresh.

The practical consequence is a schedule. Review a new fact after a day, then three days, then a week, then a month. Each successful retrieval pushes the next review further out, and the total time you spend is a fraction of what a cramming session costs.

Cramming, by contrast, works for the exam on Tuesday and fails for the exam in June. The difference is not effort but timing: massed practice builds fluency without durability, and fluency is easy to mistake for knowledge.

None of this requires software, flashcards or a system. It requires that you stop reading and start recalling, and that you let a little forgetting happen in between.
